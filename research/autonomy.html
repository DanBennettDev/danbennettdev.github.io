User Autonomy

The main strand of my research addresses autonomous motivation in technology use.

Experiences of agency and autonomy are central to our enjoyment, motivation and well-being. However, these experiences are not always easy to understand: The term “autonomy” gathers together “big”, lasting issues such as identity and choosing our path in life, alongside smaller, more passing experiences such as a sense of power and control in a game, or choice and self-expression in a social media feed.

How do these various aspects of agency and autonomy interconnect in technology use? Which aspects really matter - for example, influencing our motivation in tasks that matter to our lives? And can technologies manipulate us by making us feel in control, when we are really acting against our interests?

I apply perspectives from psychology and philosophy to clarify how technologies can support autonomy, and to understand the conditions that undermine it. I investigate how technologies can promote motivation and competency development in areas that people truly value, understand and pursue their own goals, and navigate the “ultra-processed environments” of the attention economy.

A particularly important part of this work concerns motivational design: how technologies can promote what psychologists call “autonomous motivation”: a healthy value-centred basis for engagement in activities like health, cyber-security and education that are important but not always easy or immediately rewarding.
